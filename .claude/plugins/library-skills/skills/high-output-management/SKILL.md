# High Output Management — Andy Grove

## Core frame

A manager's output is the output of their organization plus the output of neighboring organizations under their influence — so management is a production problem. The four-part argument: (1) any work can be modeled as production (inputs → limiting step → output, with indicators); (2) the manager's job is leverage — concentrate time on activities that scale across people or persist over time; (3) every real organization is a hybrid of mission and function held together by dual reporting and three modes of control; (4) motivate people via task-relevant feedback, with management style adapted to each person's task-relevant maturity (TRM). Grove's MBO is the textual source of the OKR system.

## Key concepts

- **Manager's output** — output of your org + output of neighboring orgs you influence. Not your personal activity.
- **Managerial leverage** — output produced per unit of managerial time on an activity. High-leverage activities affect many people, persist over time, or unblock others with a key insight.
- **Limiting step** — the operation that fixes the time/cost of the whole process. Schedule backward from it.
- **Indicator types** — output (lagging), leading (predicts future output), trailing (confirms past), linkage (paired indicators that catch each other's blind spots).
- **OMA accounting** — categorize activities as Output, Maintenance, or Activity. Don't confuse activity with output.
- **Task-relevant maturity (TRM)** — a person's effectiveness on *this specific task*, independent of seniority. Low → structured what-when-how; medium → communication and support; high → minimal involvement, focus on objectives.
- **Three modes of control** — free-market forces, contractual obligations, cultural values. The CUA factor (complexity, uncertainty, ambiguity) selects which mode fits.
- **Mission-oriented vs functional orgs** — every large org is a hybrid; pure forms don't survive scale.
- **Dual reporting** — Grove's operational solution to the hybrid problem; matrix reporting as practical reality, not pathology.
- **MBO / proto-OKR** — objectives (what) + key results (measurable proof). Drucker → Grove → Doerr lineage.
- **Three L's (performance review)** — Level (be candid), Listen (the report is the active learner), Leave yourself out (it's about them).
- **Peer-plus-one** — when peers can't agree on a cross-org decision, the smallest superset including their first common boss decides.
- **Free discussion → clear decision → full support** — the structure every decision should follow; commitment to support is independent of agreement.

## Heuristics

- **1:1 cadence by TRM:** low TRM → weekly, ~1 hr, report owns agenda; medium → biweekly; high → as-needed but no longer than monthly. The 1:1 is the report's meeting; you are the consultant.
- **Compute leverage before you act.** Before delegating or doing a task yourself, ask: how many people does this affect × how long does the effect last × how much does my involvement amplify it?
- **Training is the boss's job, not HR's.** One of the highest-leverage activities a manager can do; the leverage compounds with team size.
- **Indicator pairing.** Never trust a single indicator — always pair it with one that catches its failure mode (e.g., volume + quality, speed + error rate).
- **Forecasting > reacting.** Manage with leading indicators; trailing indicators are for accountability, not steering.
- **Six-question decision frame:** What decision? By when? Who decides? Who consults before? Who ratifies? Who is informed afterward? Use it explicitly on any cross-functional decision.
- **Don't promote people past their TRM.** The Peter Principle bites when you confuse senority (general experience) with maturity (effectiveness on this specific job).
- **Interview goals:** technical competence, dealing-with-failure, value match. Press on all three; reject if any fails.
- **Resignation conversation:** don't accept the first "I quit" if the person is valuable — find out the real driver and address it before the offer becomes inevitable.

## When to apply

- **"How should I run my 1:1s?"** → Set cadence by report's TRM; report owns the agenda; you ask clarifying questions and surface blockers. Frequency adapts as TRM changes per task.
- **"My report is underperforming."** → Diagnose: is this low TRM on a new task (coachable, increase involvement) or genuine performance issue (run the three-L's review process)? Don't conflate.
- **"What should I focus on as a new EM?"** → Apply OMA accounting to your week. Then identify the 2–3 highest-leverage activities and protect time for them (training, 1:1s, indicator-pairing, decisions that unblock multiple reports).
- **"How do I evaluate my team's output?"** → Use Grove's manager's-output definition: org output + neighboring-org output you influenced. Build indicators that pair lagging output measures with leading ones.
- **"How do I write OKRs?"** → Objective = one sentence, ambitious, time-bound. Key results = measurable, falsifiable, 3–5 of them. The OKR is a contract about *what success looks like*, not a task list.
- **"Matrix / dual reporting is broken."** → Confirm both reporting lines have explicit decision rights and the CUA factor justifies the matrix. If a decision keeps bouncing, escalate to peer-plus-one to avoid stalling.
- **"How do I structure this decision?"** → Free discussion (everyone airs the case) → clear decision (named owner decides) → full support (dissenters commit to execute). Use the six-question frame before launching.
- **"Should I do skip-levels?"** → Yes, for information-gathering with redundancy; no, as a routing-around tactic. Frame them explicitly as the latter to avoid undermining your direct.

## Out of scope

- **Career coaching for individual contributors** — Grove's frame is operational management; for "should I take this job" or salary negotiation, use a career-advice resource.
- **Modern remote / async team mechanics** — the 1:1 and meeting heuristics still apply but the logistics don't translate one-to-one.
- **Engineering-management specifics** Grove didn't address — on-call rotations, incident command, sprint planning. The vocabulary applies; the recipes don't.
- **Therapy-grade interpersonal issues** — Grove's frame is workplace operational, not mental-health support.

## Source

*High Output Management*, Andrew S. Grove, 1983 (Random House); 1995 reissue with new intro by Grove; 2015 reissue with foreword by Ben Horowitz. 192 pages.
