# Good Strategy / Bad Strategy — Richard Rumelt

## Core frame

Most documents called "strategy" are not strategies. A real strategy is a *coherent response to an important challenge*, built on a three-part kernel: **diagnosis** (what kind of problem is this?) → **guiding policy** (how will we deal with it?) → **coherent actions** (what specific moves implement the policy?). Bad strategy is recognisable by four hallmarks. Power comes from one or more of nine asymmetries the strategist can exploit. The book is part diagnostic instrument, part cognitive-discipline manual; the value is in the vocabulary that lets you name what's wrong with a hollow plan.

## Key concepts

- **The kernel of strategy** — diagnosis + guiding policy + coherent action. All three are required; missing one collapses the strategy.
- **Diagnosis** — naming the kind of challenge: what's actually hard about this situation? Replaces "improve performance" with "the problem is X".
- **Guiding policy** — how the organization will approach the challenge. Excludes some moves to enable others. Not a goal; not a vision; a posture.
- **Coherent actions** — concrete steps that reinforce each other and implement the policy. Coherence is the test: actions must not pull against each other.
- **Fluff** — abstract restatement of the obvious dressed up as strategy. "Be customer-centric." A red flag.
- **Failure to face the challenge** — no diagnosis. The strategy is launched into the air without a target.
- **Mistaking goals for strategy** — "we will grow 20%" is a goal, not a plan for how.
- **Bad strategic objectives** — long unprioritized lists of aspirations, or blue-sky objectives disconnected from action.
- **Nine sources of strategic power:** leverage (focused force on the pivot point), proximate objectives (close-enough-to-be-achievable next step), chain-link systems (the strength is the weakest link — fix it to unlock), design (configured advantage), focus (concentrate on a vulnerable segment), growth (only valuable when it compounds advantage), advantage (asymmetry that's hard to copy), dynamics (riding a wave others miss), inertia & entropy (incumbents are weighed down by their own past).
- **Chain-link logic** — if any one link is weak, raising the others doesn't help; identify the binding constraint and fix that.
- **Proximate objective** — the next step the team can actually do, given current resources, that moves toward the bigger goal. Too-distant objectives produce paralysis.
- **Inside view vs outside view** — the inside view (this is special) systematically overestimates success; the outside view (compared to similar situations) is more reliable.

## Heuristics

- **Run the kernel test on every strategy doc.** Find the diagnosis (one paragraph, names the challenge). Find the guiding policy (excludes moves). Find the coherent actions (concrete, mutually reinforcing). If any is missing, you don't have a strategy.
- **Name the hallmark.** When a strategy feels hollow, point at *which* hallmark — fluff, no-diagnosis, goal-as-strategy, bad-objectives. Naming the failure mode is half the critique.
- **Find the pivot point.** Before recommending a move, ask: what is the one place where focused effort cascades into disproportionate effect? That's where the leverage source applies.
- **Chain-link diagnostic:** when efforts in many areas yield little, suspect chain-link structure. Identify the weakest link. Don't dilute effort on the strong ones.
- **Reject growth-as-strategy.** Growth that doesn't compound advantage is just bigger costs. Ask: what asymmetry will the growth produce?
- **Proximate objectives over moonshots.** Set the next-step goal close enough that the team can actually do it. Then iterate.
- **Beware coherent-sounding incoherence.** Two actions can each be reasonable and still pull against each other. Test pairwise.

## When to apply

- **"Review this strategy doc."** → Run the kernel test. Find diagnosis / policy / actions. If absent, name the missing piece. Then identify which hallmark of bad strategy is at work.
- **"This strategy feels off."** → It's probably fluff or goal-as-strategy. Look for the substitution: are inspiring words doing the work of analysis? Are growth targets doing the work of a plan?
- **"How should we compete against X?"** → Diagnose: what's *their* weakness (what they can't or won't do)? What's *our* asymmetry? Then pick the source of power (focus, design, dynamics) and write the kernel.
- **"What's our advantage?"** → Don't just list features. Ask: which asymmetry would be hard for a rival to copy? Sustained advantage requires one of the nine sources — name it.
- **"We have a goal list — is that strategy?"** → No. A goal list is a goal list. Strategy is the *how* — guiding policy + coherent action. Push back on the missing kernel.
- **"Are we stuck on a chain-link problem?"** → If improvements in multiple areas don't compound, yes. Find the binding link. Fix it before anything else.
- **"How do we set objectives?"** → Use proximate objectives — close enough that the team can plausibly hit them with current resources. Moonshots paralyze; proximate objectives compound.

## Out of scope

- **Execution / operational planning** — Rumelt's frame is the *content* of strategy, not the *delivery*. For roadmaps, sprint plans, project management, use a different lens.
- **Personal goal-setting / individual career strategy** — the kernel is about organizations facing competitive challenges; doesn't translate cleanly to personal life.
- **Marketing positioning copy** — Rumelt critiques marketing-as-strategy; this skill won't help you write a positioning statement, only critique one.
- **Industry-specific playbooks** — the book teaches *how to think*, not *what to do in industry X*. Don't expect recipes.
